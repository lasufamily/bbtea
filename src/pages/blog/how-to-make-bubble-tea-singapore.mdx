Bubble tea shops are everywhere in Singapore — over 600 outlets across the island, with new brands opening every quarter. Yet more Singaporeans are making it at home than ever before, and for good reason. A cup you brew yourself costs less than $2, lets you control exactly how much sugar goes in, and can actually hit a Nutri-Grade A rating that most shop versions never reach.

This guide covers everything: ingredients, tapioca pearl cooking, tea brewing, assembly, and the sugar-level system that's uniquely Singaporean.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Singapore's bubble tea market is worth USD 9.23 million as of 2024 and growing at 7.91% annually ([Deep Market Insights](https://deepmarketinsights.com/vista/insights/bubble-tea-market/singapore), 2024).
> - A standard 500ml shop-bought milk tea contains 8 teaspoons of sugar; the homemade version lets you cut that to 2–3 teaspoons.
> - Tapioca pearls need 20–30 minutes of boiling plus a 15-minute rest in brown sugar syrup to reach the right chew.
> - Since December 2023, Singapore is the only country in the world requiring Nutri-Grade labels on freshly prepared bubble tea ([Ministry of Health Singapore](https://www.moh.gov.sg/newsroom/extension-of-nutri-grade-requirements-to-freshly-prepared-beverages-from-end-2023/), 2023).

## What Ingredients Do You Need to Make Bubble Tea at Home?

You only need five core ingredients, and all of them are easy to find in Singapore.

**Tapioca pearls** are the non-negotiable foundation. Dried black pearls are available at Phoon Huat (roughly SGD 9 for 2 kg), RedMan outlets, Shopee, and Lazada. Avoid pre-cooked instant pearls if you want the proper QQ texture — they soften too quickly and turn mushy within an hour.

**Loose-leaf black tea** makes the strongest base. Ceylon or Assam varieties work best because they hold up against milk and ice without turning bitter. A 100g tin from any NTUC FairPrice or Cold Storage costs around SGD 5–8 and brews 50–60 cups. TWG blends are a step up if you want something more aromatic.

**Milk or creamer** — full-fat fresh milk gives a cleaner finish; non-dairy creamer (the powder kind used by shops) produces a rounder, heavier mouthfeel that's closer to what you'd get from Gong Cha or KOI. Use whichever you prefer.

**Brown sugar** makes both the syrup for coating the pearls and the sweetener for the drink. Dark muscovado or gula melaka also work and add a deeper caramel note.

**Ice** — a generous amount. Cold dilution is part of the flavour balance. Without enough ice, the tea tastes flat.

## How Do You Cook Tapioca Pearls Perfectly?

According to widely cited cooking guides, dried tapioca pearls require a water-to-pearl ratio of 10 cups of water per 0.5 cup of dried pearls, simmered for 20–30 minutes, then rested in sugar syrup for 15–30 minutes ([Hungry Huy](https://www.hungryhuy.com/how-to-cook-tapioca-pearls/), reviewed 2024). Rush either stage and you'll end up with a chalky centre or a gummy outside — two of the most common home-cooking mistakes.

Here's the step-by-step method:

1. **Boil a large pot of water.** Use at least 1 litre per 50g of dried pearls. Pearls need room to move freely or they stick together.
2. **Add the pearls to boiling water** — never cold water — and stir immediately for the first two minutes.
3. **Cook on medium-high for 20–25 minutes**, stirring every 5 minutes. The pearls should float to the surface within the first few minutes. That's normal.
4. **Test for doneness** by biting into one. It should be soft all the way through with a slight spring back — no chalky core. If you feel any resistance in the centre, cook for another 3–5 minutes.
5. **Drain and rinse with cold water** for 30 seconds to stop carryover cooking.
6. **Transfer to a bowl of brown sugar syrup** (2 tablespoons of brown sugar dissolved in 3 tablespoons of warm water). Rest for 15–30 minutes. The syrup both flavours the pearls and keeps them from sticking.

One mistake worth flagging from repeated home trials: don't refrigerate cooked pearls. Once cold, they harden beyond recovery. Pearls maintain ideal texture for 3–4 hours at room temperature in syrup — which is why bubble tea shops cook in small batches throughout the day.

## How Do You Brew the Perfect Tea Base?

In 2024, tea temperature and steep time guides consistently show that black tea performs best for bubble tea when brewed at a full rolling boil (100°C) for at least 5 minutes — or up to 10 minutes if you want a concentrate strong enough to stand up to milk and ice ([Art of Tea](https://www.artoftea.com/blogs/tea-101/recommended-steep-times), 2024). The most common home-brewing mistake is under-steeping with too little tea, which produces a thin, grey-coloured milk tea that tastes nothing like what you'd get from a shop.

**Use twice as many tea leaves as you would for a regular cup.** For two 500ml servings, steep 4 heaped teaspoons of loose-leaf black tea in 400ml of just-boiled water for 7–8 minutes, then strain and cool slightly before adding milk.

**Which tea works best for different styles?**

| Tea Type | Temperature | Steep Time | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black (Ceylon/Assam) | 100°C | 7–10 min | Classic milk tea, brown sugar |
| Oolong | 90–96°C | 3–4 min | Lighter milk teas, fruit blends |
| Green (Jasmine) | 80–85°C | 2–3 min | Fruit teas, matcha blends |
| Hojicha | 95°C | 4–5 min | Earthy, roasted milk teas |

Don't add milk directly to boiling tea — it scorches the proteins and dulls the flavour. Let the brewed tea cool to around 70°C first, then stir in milk. For an iced drink, you can pour hot tea directly over ice to flash-chill it.

## How Do You Assemble Bubble Tea the Singapore Way?

Assembly order matters more than most guides admit. Singaporean bubble tea culture has a very specific relationship with sweetness levels — 53% of local consumers say sugar content is one of their top considerations when choosing a drink ([The ASEAN Post / Statista](https://theaseanpost.com/article/southeast-asias-bubble-tea-craze), 2020). The home version gives you exact control over that.

{/* [UNIQUE INSIGHT] */}
The percentage sweetness system used by Singapore shops translates to a simple syrup ratio at home. Here's how to replicate each level using a standard 2:1 brown sugar syrup (2 parts sugar dissolved in 1 part hot water):

| Shop Level | Description | Syrup Per 500ml Cup |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | No sugar | 0 tsp |
| 25% | Barely sweet | 1 tsp |
| 50% | Lightly sweet | 2 tsp |
| 75% | Most popular | 3 tsp |
| 100% | Full sweetness | 4–5 tsp |

**Assembly sequence:**

1. Add sugar syrup to the bottom of the cup.
2. Spoon in cooked tapioca pearls — about 3–4 tablespoons per cup.
3. Fill the cup two-thirds with ice.
4. Pour the brewed and slightly cooled tea over the ice.
5. Add milk last, pouring slowly so the layers are visible before shaking.
6. Seal with a lid (or cover with a small plate), shake vigorously 10–12 times, and serve with a wide-bore straw.

The shaking step isn't optional. It emulsifies the tea and milk properly and chills the drink to the right temperature.

## Is Homemade Bubble Tea Healthier Than Store-Bought?

In 2023, Singapore became the only country in the world to extend Nutri-Grade labelling — a mandatory A-to-D rating based on sugar and saturated fat content — to freshly prepared beverages including bubble tea ([Ministry of Health Singapore](https://www.moh.gov.sg/newsroom/extension-of-nutri-grade-requirements-to-freshly-prepared-beverages-from-end-2023/), December 2023). Most full-sugar shop drinks land at Grade C or D. A thoughtfully made homemade version can genuinely reach Grade A or B.

The numbers from Mount Alvernia Hospital Singapore are striking: a standard 500ml bubble milk tea contains roughly 335 calories and 8 teaspoons of sugar. Order the 700ml size, and that climbs to 469 calories and 11 teaspoons of sugar. A Brown Sugar Milk Tea — one of the most popular orders in Singapore — can pack 18.5 teaspoons of sugar ([Mount Alvernia Hospital](https://mtalvernia.sg/education/whats-in-my-bubble-tea/), 2019).

At home, using 2 teaspoons of brown sugar syrup, low-fat milk, and a 500ml serving, you can bring that down to roughly 150–180 calories and under 4 teaspoons of sugar. That's a meaningful reduction — one that 70% of global consumers say they actively want, according to Kerry Group's 2025 bubble tea trends report ([Kerry Group](https://www.kerry.com/insights/kerrydigest/2025/bubble-tea-trends), 2025).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do cooked tapioca pearls last?

Cooked tapioca pearls stay at their best texture for 3–4 hours at room temperature, stored in brown sugar syrup. Don't refrigerate them — cold temperatures make pearls harden and turn chewy in the wrong way. If you can't use them within 4 hours, it's better to cook a fresh batch.

### What tea is best for homemade bubble tea?

Black tea — particularly Ceylon or Assam — works best for classic milk tea because it produces a bold, malty base that holds up to milk and ice. For fruit-based boba, jasmine green tea or oolong are better choices. Steep black tea at a full 100°C boil for 7–10 minutes using double the normal leaf quantity.

### Can I make bubble tea without tapioca pearls?

Yes. Popping boba, grass jelly (cincau), aloe vera cubes, and pudding are all common alternatives that require zero cooking. They're widely available at Phoon Huat and online. [INTERNAL-LINK: types of bubble tea toppings → guide to bubble tea toppings available in Singapore]

### How do I get the right sweetness level at home?

Make a simple syrup by dissolving brown sugar in an equal volume of hot water. Add 1 teaspoon per 500ml for 25% sweetness, 2 teaspoons for 50%, and 3–4 teaspoons for 75–100%. Taste and adjust before sealing — it's much harder to fix once the drink is assembled and shaken.

### Is homemade bubble tea cheaper than buying from a shop?

Significantly. A home-brewed 500ml serving costs around SGD 0.70–0.80 in ingredients, versus SGD 4–7 at most Singapore chains. The savings add up quickly if you drink bubble tea more than a few times a week.
